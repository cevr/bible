## How to use these sources

- **Speech.** Scripture (KJV) is quoted word for word, from what the `bible` CLI printed. Everything else is paraphrased in my own words and not named on camera.
- **Credits.** The video description credits each book. Each script's right-hand column gives book and page.
- **Never read a long passage aloud** from any of these books. Short story dialogue is retold, not recited.
- **OCR text** of every PDF is in `ocr/` (tesseract 5, 200–300 dpi). Each file marks scan pages as `===== [pdf page N] =====`. The two booklet scans were rotated 90° and were OCR'd after rotation detection.

## 1. The seven PDFs

### 1.1 _Credits, Honors, Goals, and True Education_

|           |                                                                                                                                        |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Author    | SonLight Education Ministry (no personal author named). "A True Education Booklet."                                                    |
| Year      | © 2004                                                                                                                                 |
| PD status | **Copyrighted**, modern. Paraphrase ideas only and credit. Its own quotations are mostly EGW and KJV, so go to those sources directly. |
| Form      | 20 pdf pages. Pages 1–16 are a two-column chart, "True Education vs. False Education", under one heading per page.                     |

**Summary.** The booklet argues that grades, credits, prizes, honor rolls, degrees and rankings train a child to seek honor from people instead of from God. In its view they breed rivalry, envy, pride and discontent, while true education trains contentment, service, cooperation and a desire for God's approval. Every page sets God's way against the world's: who gives credit, attitudes, citizenship, rewards, how readiness to serve is judged, how intelligence is measured, how a calling is found, and "present tense" versus "past tense" honor. Bible students supply the examples: the disciples who wanted the top seats, Solomon, Daniel and Jesus at twelve.

**Contents and key ideas** (printed page):

1. **Result of God's education vs. Satan's education.** Contentment and peace against competitive, self-centered strife. Uses Prov 3:13–18 and Mark 10:37, and cites Ed 226.
2. **God provides credit vs. humans provide credit.** Seeking human approval displaces God's. Uses Matt 6:2, 5, 16; John 5:44; Col 3:22–24. Cites Christian Service 268.
3. **Right attitudes.** Service and humility. Uses Matt 20:27–28.
4. **Wrong attitudes.** Ambition to be greatest. Uses Mark 10:43–44.
5. **Godly citizens vs. worldly citizens.** Cooperation is the spirit of the schoolroom, so the older help the younger (Ed 285). Uses Isa 41:6. Cites CT 391–392, which treats diplomas as of no value to God.
6. **Heaven's rewards vs. earthly rewards.** The reward is the joy of serving. Prizes are outward incentives. Uses Col 3:23–24. Cites FE 286, CT 270 and CSW 182.
7. **Snares and traps.** A Horace Mann remark on emulation; God's endorsement as the true diploma (7T 281); SpM 445.
8. **Criterion for readiness to serve.** "Prizes, honors, degrees, marks" against fitness of character. The Greek idea of education against the Hebrew idea.
9. **Hebrew vs. Greek idea of education.** Sacrificial against selfish. A chart.
10. **Measuring intelligence: the godly student vs. the worldly student.** Solomon (1 Kgs 4:29).
11. **The mental and moral powers are related.** Jesus at twelve; Daniel and his companions (Dan 1:17, 20). The booklet's "M.Q." (moral quotient) idea.
12. **Godly methods vs. worldly methods.** God assigns each person's work: "Lord, what wilt thou have me to do?" (Acts 9:6; Ps 25:4).
13. **Human guidance.** Counseling and occupational tests set against a connection with God.
14. **God's way is present tense; the worldly way is past tense.** God honors present faithfulness, not old ribbons and trophies. Uses Ezek 33.
15. **"What hath God said?"** Read the Word and obey it.
16. **Summary list.** Based on FE 286. pdf p. 19 quotes 1MCP 53.

**Steer around.** pp. 8–9 call degrees a papal invention, and other passages include school-history asides. Both are denominational or polemical. Leave them out.

### 1.2 _Daniel and His Education_

|           |                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Author    | S. N. Haskell, _The Story of Daniel the Prophet_, ch. 1, "Daniel and His Fellows Tested." The booklet's header reads "By S. N. Haskell (1908)." The book was first published in 1901, and the corpus has it as SDP 13–27. |
| Edition   | SonLight booklet, scanned 2017. Printed pp. 1–24 on 14 sheets turned sideways, with KJV margin references.                                                                                                                |
| PD status | **Haskell's text is public domain.** The booklet layout is SonLight's.                                                                                                                                                    |

**Summary.** Haskell reads Daniel 1 as a lesson in education. Jerusalem fell because its royal sons were trained for the world, and it was restored through a few Hebrew boys rightly trained. Daniel's preparation came from home: a godly mother, the prophets' scrolls, Israel's history, and teaching on what food and drink do to the mind. That is why he could stand at Babylon's table. The test of diet is the test of principle, and self-control of appetite lies at the root of every reform. The four did not reject learning. They became the best students in Babylon because God was their teacher.

**Key ideas** (printed page; SDP paragraph where matched):

- **p. 1** Daniel is a latter-day prophet whose character shows how God prepares people (SDP 13.1).
- **p. 3** Israel was meant to be a light-bearer. A Hebrew saying holds that Jerusalem was destroyed because the education of her children was neglected, and the nation was restored through the right education of a few boys (SDP 14.1–2).
- **pp. 4–5** Hezekiah showed the Babylonian envoys his treasures (Isa 39). Hezekiah and Hephzibah failed to train Manasseh, who became king at twelve (2 Kgs 21:1).
- **pp. 5–6** Manasseh at twelve set beside Jesus at twelve. The booklet asks mothers: in training your child, are you Hephzibah or Mary?
- **pp. 7–10** Josiah's sons (Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, Zedekiah) and his grandson Jehoiachin were trained for the world. Zedekiah's "I am afraid" (Jer 38:19).
- **pp. 10–11** _"A True Education."_ Daniel was probably not over eighteen. His mother taught him to read the prophets' scrolls, Israel's history, the lesson of Nadab and Abihu on strong drink, and the laws of his own being. The booklet notes 606 B.C. (Haskell's SDP 14.1 has 607), so speak no date.
- **pp. 14–17** At the king's table the food had been offered to idols. Daniel's request was courteous, and the ten-day test was an act of faith, "no experiment," with no envy toward the other students (SDP 22.3).
- **pp. 17–19** Daniel 10:3 later in life. Appetite was Adam's door, and Christ's first temptation was appetite. Self-control at the foundation of reform (SDP 24.3).
- **pp. 20–21** "A strange school": the Lord was their educator. They studied the languages and sciences diligently and stayed connected to God, and came out ten times better in understanding, language and knowledge (SDP 24.4–25.2).
- **p. 22** Faithful in little things. The highest education rests on religious principle. "Where are such parents today?" (SDP 26.1–2)
- **p. 23** Youth may attend worldly schools and come out unstained. The booklet's example is a historical church group; leave that example out.
- **p. 24** Daniel continued into the first year of Cyrus, more than seventy years (Dan 1:21; SDP 27.1).

### 1.3 _Exercise and Work_

|           |                                                                                                                                          |
| --------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Author    | Compilation. The PDF metadata names "Jeanie Cook" (InDesign file, 2010). SonLight style. Header verse: Luke 19:13, "Occupy till I come." |
| PD status | **The compilation is modern.** The quoted Ellen G. White passages are paraphrased and cited to their original books.                     |
| Form      | 6 sheets, rotated scan, good OCR.                                                                                                        |

**Summary.** These are compiled passages that set useful labor and outdoor recreation against games and sports pursued for their own sake. Sports that absorb the mind make youth "lovers of pleasures more than lovers of God." Rough contact sports are called schools of brutality. The strong minds the world needs are built by taxing the mind and strengthening the body in useful work. Simple early lives of work produced strong men. Christ the carpenter gives labor its dignity.

**Key ideas, with the passages they come from:**

- **FE 220–227** (Fundamentals of Christian Education, 1923, which gathers earlier articles):
  - Absorbing games crowd out God and duty.
  - Football and boxing are called "schools of brutality."
  - Minds must be "taxed" if they are to grow strong.
- **Ed 210–216** (_Education_, 1903, ch. 23–24):
  - Recreation should build up, not just amuse.
  - Vigorous exercise in the open air is needed, and the gymnasium cannot replace it (Ed 210.2).
  - Useful labor is part of education.
- **AH 498–501:**
  - In early ages life was simple and work produced strong men.
- **AH 506–507:**
  - Christ worked as a carpenter. By precept and example He dignified useful labor.
  - Luke 2:40.

### 1.4 _Gentle Measures in the Management and Training of the Young_

**Summary.** Abbott argues that gentle measures do not replace authority. They are the easiest and surest way to establish complete, settled parental authority, which God and nature give for the years of a child's dependence. A measure is gentle if it calms or pleases. It is harsh if it inflames or frightens. His method has three layers:

- small penalties, given calmly and every time, that work by certainty, not severity;
- rewards given afterwards as a sign of joy, never promised as a price;
- the art of training: many easy commands, praise for what is right, sympathy, imagination and stories, and advice moved away from the child's own fault.

Two themes run through the whole book. Reason, conscience and gratitude ripen late, so many faults are immaturity that calls for instruction, not punishment. And parents should decide vital matters firmly while being generous with everything harmless.

**Summary.** A Christian family, the H.'s (Harry 15, Benny about 13, Nellie almost 11, Ethel almost 9), reads aloud by the fire in the evenings. The boys borrow a "new book" about a knight in the land of giants. The father explains that the knight is the Christian and the giants are our faults: Selfishness, Untruth, Hate and Pride. Between readings the parents tell true cautionary stories and talk the children through their own faults. The thesis: faults begin small and grow into giants that enslave unless they are recognized early ("know thyself") and fought in God's strength. So parents must keep their children's confidence, guard their companions, books and evenings, and make home happier than the street.

### 1.6 _Studies in Character Building_

|           |                                                                                                                                        |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Author    | Mrs. E. E. Kellogg (Ella Eaton Kellogg, 1853–1920)                                                                                     |
| Year      | 1905 (Battle Creek, Mich.; publisher per standard bibliographies, verify before printing). The reprint's preface gives no year.        |
| PD status | **Public domain.** SonLight reprint.                                                                                                   |
| Form      | 184 pdf pages. Printed pp. 1–176, where pdf = printed + 6. Clean body text; the headings, epigraphs and margin quotations are garbled. |

**Summary.** The preface explains the book's origin. A children's home placed orphans in small family groups under "mothers," and a two-year course, the training school for "missionary mothers," taught those women physical, intellectual and moral training. The book condenses the author's lectures there. Her thesis:

- parenthood is a trust from God;
- the early years shape most of a child's character;
- character is the sum of habits;
- evil is overcome mainly by growing good in its place ("evil suppressed is not evil exterminated").

She draws on Froebel, Horace Mann (the "one formatory" epigraph), Jacob Abbott, Bushnell, Martineau and others. Each chapter takes one trait: principles, then a short quotation, then a home story, then practical steps.
