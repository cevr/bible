# Deck 3 §2 — Worldview Fairness Check: "Why Suffering?"

**Task G6** — For a skeptical audience that may include adherents of each view. Goal: characterize each
rival answer _accurately and respectfully_ (no strawmen) before giving the Bible's. The deck's test is
**"explains AND fixes"** — does the worldview both account for suffering's ORIGIN and credibly promise its END?

Source policy reminder: on-slide citations = Scripture, named scholars, encyclopedias, primary texts.
EGW/pioneer material shapes framing only (none cited here). Everything below is slide-citable.

---

## The framing rule for the whole section

State the criterion ONCE, up front, so it does not feel like a rigged game: _"A complete answer to
suffering should do two things — explain where it came from, and offer a real end to it. Let's hold every
view, including the Bible's, to the same two questions."_ Then apply it evenly. This is the fair move; the
audience watches whether you score everyone on the same scale.

**Do NOT** rank these as "wrong religions." Each contains genuine insight. The honest claim is narrower:
most either explain origin OR promise an end, but few claim a _cosmic, guaranteed, external_ end to
suffering itself. That is the distinctive the Bible's answer is making — not "we're smart, they're dumb."

---

## 1. Secular atheism / materialism

**1. Fair one-sentence answer:** On materialism there is no "why" — suffering is a purposeless byproduct of
a universe that, in Richard Dawkins's words, has "no design, no purpose, no evil and no good, nothing but
blind pitiless indifference," in which pain is simply what evolved nervous systems do (Dawkins, _River Out
of Eden_, 1995, ch. 4).

**2. Honest limitation (speaker line):** This is the _most intellectually honest_ view in one respect — it
refuses to pretend suffering means something — but on its own terms it neither explains _why this rather
than nothing_ nor offers any END; entropy and death are final, so it diagnoses without curing.

**3. Phrasing to avoid:** Do NOT say atheists "have no morals" or "no reason to care about suffering" —
that is false and insulting; materialists care about and fight suffering intensely (medicine, justice,
charity). The fair point is only that their _metaphysics_ supplies no cosmic meaning or final remedy, not
that _they_ are heartless. Avoid the smug "if there's no God why does anything matter" jab.

---

## 2. Buddhism

**1. Fair one-sentence answer:** Suffering (_dukkha_) is the pervasive unsatisfactoriness of all
conditioned existence, and its origin is craving/attachment (_tanha_) rooted in ignorance (_avijja_); it
ends by extinguishing craving via the Noble Eightfold Path, reaching _nirvana_ (Four Noble Truths;
_Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta_ / Buddha's first sermon; cf. Britannica, "Four Noble Truths").

**2. Honest limitation (speaker line):** Buddhism uniquely takes both questions seriously — it explains the
mechanism of suffering AND maps a path out — but its "fix" is the dissolution of the craving (and
ultimately the bounded self) rather than the rescue of the person _and_ the restoration of the world; the
cure works by letting go of the very attachments love is made of.

**3. Phrasing to avoid:** Do NOT call Buddhism "nihilistic," "pessimistic," or say it teaches "life is
suffering, so give up" — scholars stress the view is _realistic_, not pessimistic, and _nirvana_ is
release, not annihilation in the despairing sense. Do not caricature non-attachment as "not caring about
people"; compassion (_karuna_) is central.

---

## 3. Hinduism (karma / samsara)

**1. Fair one-sentence answer:** Suffering is the just working-out of _karma_ — moral cause-and-effect
across many lives — within the cycle of rebirth (_samsara_) driven by ignorance (_avidya_) and desire, and
it ends only at _moksha_, liberation/union with Brahman that breaks the cycle (Britannica, "Hinduism:
Karma, samsara, and moksha"; cf. _Bhagavad Gita_ on the imperishable Atman).

**2. Honest limitation (speaker line):** Karma gives suffering a precise _explanation_ — nothing is random,
everything is earned — and it does promise an END in _moksha_; the honest tension is that the explanation
can leave present suffering looking _deserved_ (the sufferer is reaping prior-life acts), which many find
hard to square with compassion for, say, a suffering child.

**3. Phrasing to avoid:** Do NOT reduce karma to "you had it coming" or use the caste/"they earned their
suffering" framing as a gotcha — it is offensive and theologically crude; thoughtful Hindus emphasize
_response_ to suffering (wisdom, dharma, reducing others' suffering) as itself karmically central. Avoid
implying Hindus don't help sufferers because "it interferes with karma" — false.

---

## 4. Islam (test / qadar)

**1. Fair one-sentence answer:** Suffering is a _test_ (_ibtila_) permitted within God's all-encompassing
decree (_qadar_) — "He created death and life to test you, which of you is best in deed" (Qur'an 67:2) —
not arbitrary cruelty but an occasion for patience (_sabr_), reward, and growth, with full justice
delivered in the hereafter (St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology, "Evil and Suffering" [Islam]; Qur'an
2:155, 64:11).

**2. Honest limitation (speaker line):** Islam clearly _explains_ suffering (a meaningful test under a wise
God) AND promises an END (paradise, perfect justice hereafter) — so it answers both; the point to state
honestly is that the _resolution is deferred entirely to the next life_, and on questions of _why God
decrees this specific suffering_, the tradition itself counsels reverent silence ("He is not questioned
about what He does," Qur'an 21:23).

**3. Phrasing to avoid:** Do NOT call Islam "fatalistic" or say Muslims believe "everything is predestined
so free will is fake" — mainstream Sunni theology (Ash'ari, Maturidi) explicitly affirms human moral
responsibility _alongside_ qadar and denies God "loves" evil. Avoid the "Allah is capricious" trope; the
scholarly position is God acts from wisdom, even where hidden.

---

## 5. Generic New-Age ("suffering is illusion / vibration")

**1. Fair one-sentence answer:** In the popular New-Age synthesis, suffering is largely _unreal or
self-generated_ — a product of identifying with ego/mind and resisting the present ("the pain-body,"
Eckhart Tolle, _The Power of Now_, 1997) or of low "vibration"/wrong thinking — and it dissolves through
awakened consciousness, presence, or alignment.

**2. Honest limitation (speaker line):** This view offers the most _immediate-feeling_ "fix" — change your
consciousness now — but it struggles most with _real, external_ suffering: if a child's starvation or a
genocide is "illusion" or "low vibration," the framework either has to deny the suffering is real or imply
the sufferer attracted it, which is exactly where critics (and most sufferers) find it breaks down.

**3. Phrasing to avoid:** Do NOT lump all meditative or mindfulness practice into "it's all fake" mockery —
many find genuine relief in presence-practices, and the perception point (much suffering is amplified by
mind) is partly true. The _fair_ critique is narrow and should be stated as critics state it: the
"suffering is illusion / you attract it" claim becomes **victim-blaming and dismissive** when applied to
trauma, injustice, or mental illness (this is the documented mainstream criticism, e.g., of Tolle's
pain-body theory — cite the critique, not a strawman). Avoid mocking "vibrations" as a cheap laugh line; it
alienates the very seekers in the room.

## The Bible's answer (the contrast the section is building toward)

State it to the SAME two-question test, not as a trump card:

- **Explains the ORIGIN:** Suffering and death are _intruders_, not original features — they entered through
  the Fall, a real rupture between humanity and God: _"by one man sin entered into the world, and death by
  sin"_ (Romans 5:12); the ground itself was cursed _"for thy sake... in sorrow shalt thou eat of it"_
  (Genesis 3:17–19). So suffering is neither illusion, nor deserved karmic debt, nor mere physics — it is a
  wound in a good creation.
- **Promises the END — externally and cosmically:** _"The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death"_
  (1 Corinthians 15:26); _"God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death,
  neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away"_
  (Revelation 21:4).

**The distinctive (state it humbly):** every view above either (a) explains origin without a guaranteed,
external END, or (b) offers escape/transcendence that requires letting go of the self, deferring entirely to
the afterlife, or denying the suffering is real. The Bible's claim is unusual in promising that suffering
itself is _abolished_ — the _person_ is kept, the _world_ is restored, and the _end_ is delivered by
Someone outside the system. That is a _claim to be weighed_, not a proof — but it is a genuinely different
shape of answer.

## Epicurus trilemma — attribution + slide-ready wording

**Attribution (be careful here — skeptics WILL know this):** The "trilemma" survives only through the
Christian apologist **Lactantius**, _De Ira Dei_ (_On the Wrath of God_), ch. 13 (c. 313 CE), who attributes
it to Epicurus in a single unsourced line written ~600 years after Epicurus. It appears in **no surviving
work of Epicurus**. Philologist **Reinhold F. Glei (1988,** \*Vigiliae Christianae**\*)** argued the form is
Academic-skeptic, not Epicurean — likely **Carneades** (2nd c. BCE), with a similar argument in **Sextus
Empiricus**. (Hume later popularized it via Bayle, working only from Lactantius.)

**Speaker-note caveat to use on/under the slide:** _"Traditionally called the 'Epicurean' paradox, though
it survives only in Lactantius and scholars doubt Epicurus actually authored it — the challenge stands on
its own merit regardless of who first phrased it."_ This protects you: if you label it baldly "Epicurus
said," an informed skeptic can call it a misattribution and the audience discounts the whole slide. Frame
it as a famous _argument_, not a verified quotation.

**Clean slide-ready wording (compressed from the Lactantius text):**

> **The Riddle (traditionally ascribed to Epicurus, via Lactantius, _De Ira Dei_ 13):**
> Is God willing to prevent suffering, but not able? Then He is not all-powerful.
> Is He able, but not willing? Then He is not all-good.
> Is He both able and willing? Then where does suffering come from?
> Is He neither able nor willing? Then why call Him God?

(Faithful to the four-horned structure of the original quotation; the "where does evil come from?" line is
verbatim in substance from Lactantius.)

**Why this belongs in §2:** it is the sharpest secular statement of the problem, and putting it on a slide
_for_ the skeptic signals you are not dodging the hard form. The whole section then answers it — origin (the
Fall: God did not author suffering; a free creation that could love could also fall) and end (Rev 21:4: He
is _willing and able, and is in fact removing it_, on a timeline). Lead with their strongest punch, then
answer it.
