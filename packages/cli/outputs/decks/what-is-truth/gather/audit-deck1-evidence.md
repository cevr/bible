## 2. Stanford Prison Experiment

**VERDICT: DROP** (already the plan's decision — confirmed correct).

The SPE is not a usable evidence point. Archival work by Thibault Le Texier (2018 book
_Histoire d'un mensonge_; 2019 _American Psychologist_ article) showed the guards were
coached toward brutality, conclusions were drafted in advance, and there was no control
group. Zimbardo himself conceded it was "a demonstration," not an experiment. It has never
been successfully replicated. For a truth-and-evidence deck, leaning on a study that is
itself a cautionary tale about non-replicable claims would be self-defeating.

[notes] If challenged on why it was dropped: "We pulled it on purpose — the Stanford
Prison Experiment was discredited after 2018 when researcher Thibault Le Texier found the
guards had been coached and the conclusions written in advance; it was never replicable.
We only build on evidence that survives scrutiny — which is the whole point of tonight."

**Sources**

- Thibault Le Texier, "Debunking the Stanford Prison Experiment," _American Psychologist_,
  2019 — https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/2019-letexier.pdf
- "The infamous Stanford prison experiment was flawed," _The Conversation_, 2025 —
  https://theconversation.com/the-infamous-stanford-prison-experiment-was-flawed-so-why-is-it-still-so-influential-today-246881

## 3. Milgram obedience

**VERDICT: KEEP** (with Burger 2009 as the modern anchor — defensible and replicable).

Burger (2009) ran an IRB-approved partial replication ("the 150-volt solution") and found
**70%** of participants continued past the first protest, statistically indistinguishable
from Milgram's comparable **82.5%** from 45 years earlier. The core finding — ordinary
people obey an authority figure even when they believe they are harming someone — holds.

Slide-ready:

> **Milgram (1961) → Burger (2009): ~65–70% of ordinary people will obey an authority
> figure even when they believe they're harming a stranger.**
> — Burger, _American Psychologist_, 2009 (replication of Milgram, 1963)

[notes] Honest caveat for a skeptic: Burger's version stopped at 150 volts and screened
participants, so critics call it "obedience lite." Frame it as "even a gentler, modern,
ethically-bound version produced the same obedience" — which is actually stronger for the
argument, not weaker.

**Sources**

- Jerry M. Burger, "Replicating Milgram: Would People Still Obey Today?", _American
  Psychologist_, 2009 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19209958/
- Elms, "Obedience Lite," _American Psychologist_, 2009 —
  https://elms.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/98/2014/07/2009-Obedience-Lite-Amer-Psychologist-pub.pdf

## 4. Bystander effect

**VERDICT: REPLACE** (do NOT state the naïve "people freeze and nobody helps" version —
it's been substantially overturned for real-world conflicts).

The classic Darley & Latané (1968) lab finding still holds for _non-dangerous, ambiguous_
emergencies. But the largest real-world test — Philpot et al. (2019), 219 CCTV-recorded
public conflicts across the UK, Netherlands, and South Africa — found at least one
bystander intervened in **over 90% (91%)** of cases, and _more_ bystanders made
intervention _more_ likely, not less. Fischer et al.'s 2011 meta-analysis already showed
the effect weakens or reverses in dangerous emergencies.

For this deck the honest, still-usable point is NOT "the bystander effect proves human
apathy." It's the diffusion-of-responsibility _mechanism_:

> **The "diffusion of responsibility": in a crowd, each person assumes someone else will
> act — so individual responsibility quietly evaporates. (Newer research shows people DO
> usually intervene in real danger — but only once someone owns the moment.)**
> — Darley & Latané, 1968; Philpot et al., _American Psychologist_, 2019

[notes] If you keep this at all, keep it as a metaphor for "shared/relative morality
diffuses individual moral responsibility," and be ready to concede the 2019 update. If you
want zero exposure, Milgram alone carries the "authority/conformity warps moral behavior"
load and you can drop bystander entirely. Recommended: keep the _concept_ with the caveat,
or drop — do not assert the debunked strong form.

**Sources**

- Philpot et al., "Would I Be Helped? Cross-National CCTV Footage Shows That Intervention
  Is the Norm in Public Conflicts," _American Psychologist_, 2019 —
  https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/06/190626125049.htm
- "New Study Suggests Bystander Apathy Is Not the Norm," _Psychology Today_, 2019 —
  https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/bias-fundamentals/201907/new-study-suggests-bystander-apathy-is-not-the-norm
- Bystander effect overview (incl. Fischer 2011 meta-analysis), Wikipedia —
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bystander_effect

## 5. "Over half of all marriages end in divorce"

**VERDICT: REPLACE** (the 50% claim is a debunked myth; an honest skeptic will catch it).

The "50%" figure came from flawed math (divorces-in-a-year ÷ marriages-in-a-year — not the
same people) and from peak-divorce-era (late-1970s/early-1980s) projections. Current
data: the crude divorce rate is at a ~50-year low (~2.3–2.4 per 1,000 people, 2024), and
the Institute for Family Studies projects roughly **40% of first marriages** end in
divorce — and just **15%** of marriages formed 2010–2012 had ended after a full decade.

Defensible replacement (if a "relationships are fragile / we struggle to keep our word"
point is needed):

> **About 40% of first marriages are projected to end in divorce — and the rate is at a
> 50-year low.** [notes] So even our most-considered lifelong promises fail roughly
> 4 times in 10.
> — Institute for Family Studies, 2024; CDC/NCHS, 2024

[notes] Avoid "over half." If the slide's rhetorical job was "humans can't keep promises,"
40% still makes the point honestly and you've pre-empted the fact-check. Better still: lead
with the IFS 40% number and note it's the _optimistic, corrected_ figure — that signals
intellectual honesty to a skeptic.

**Sources**

- "Half of Marriages End in Divorce?", Snopes, Aug 2024 —
  https://www.snopes.com/news/2024/08/01/marriage-divorce-rate/
- Institute for Family Studies analysis via Divorce.law, "US Divorce Rate Hits 50-Year

## 6. Internet / GPS / microwave "invented for war"

**VERDICT: REPLACE** (claims are essentially TRUE but the word "invented for war" overreaches
on the microwave; tighten to "born out of military research").

- **Internet (ARPANET):** TRUE. Funded by DARPA (Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency),
  a US DoD agency; ARPANET (1969) was the direct ancestor of the internet. Defensible.
- **GPS:** TRUE. Developed by the US Department of Defense as a military satellite-navigation
  system (NAVSTAR), opened to civilian use later. Defensible.
- **Microwave oven:** NEEDS NUANCE. It was NOT designed as a weapon. It was an _accidental
  spinoff_ of WWII radar: Raytheon engineer Percy Spencer noticed a candy bar melt near an
  active magnetron (the radar tube), and Raytheon patented the cooking oven in Oct 1945.
  So: "a peacetime accident from wartime radar technology," not "invented for war."

Slide-ready:

> **The internet (DARPA/ARPANET), GPS (US Dept. of Defense), and the microwave oven (a
> peacetime accident from WWII radar) all grew out of military research — built for one
> purpose, they ended up serving everyone.**

[notes] The honest framing actually serves the deck's later analogy better ("something
created for one purpose can have a far greater, benevolent reach"). Don't say the microwave
was "invented for war" — say it was a byproduct of the radar magnetron. A skeptic who knows
the Percy-Spencer story will respect the precision.

**Sources**

- "Who Invented the Microwave Oven?", HISTORY —
  https://www.history.com/articles/microwave-oven-invention
- Percy Spencer / cavity magnetron, Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Spencer
- (GPS/ARPANET origins are uncontroversial; DoD/DARPA provenance is standard reference.)

## 7. Kilogram drift + 2019 SI redefinition + metre via speed of light

**VERDICT: KEEP** (all details verified accurate).

- The International Prototype Kilogram (IPK), a platinum-iridium cylinder, **drifted ~50
  micrograms** relative to its official copies over ~100+ years. Verified.
- On **20 May 2019**, the kilogram (and ampere, kelvin, mole) was redefined in terms of
  fixed fundamental constants — the kilogram via the **Planck constant**
  (h = 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J·s). The IPK was the _last_ SI base unit defined by a physical
  artifact. Verified.
- The **metre** is defined via the **speed of light** (distance light travels in
  1/299,792,458 of a second); the **second** via a caesium atomic transition. Verified.

Slide-ready:

> **For 130 years the kilogram WAS a metal cylinder in a Paris vault — until scientists
> found it was slowly changing (~50 micrograms). In 2019 they replaced every physical
> "standard" with unchanging constants of nature: the metre is now defined by the speed of
> light, the kilogram by Planck's constant.**

[notes] Perfect setup for the criterion-1 point ("an absolute standard cannot drift with
the object that defines it; it must be anchored to something unchanging"). The science is
clean — cite BIPM / the 2019 SI revision.

**Sources**

- "2019 revision of the SI," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_revision_of_the_SI
- "Kilogram," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilogram
- NPL, "The redefinition of the SI units" —
  https://www.npl.co.uk/resources/the-si-units/the-redefinition-of-the-si-units

## 8. Manuscript counts (NT, OT, Iliad, Gallic Wars)

**VERDICT: REPLACE** (NT is fine; OT "17,000+" is indefensible; Iliad 643 is outdated;
Gallic Wars needs a caveat).

**New Testament — KEEP:** "5,800+ Greek manuscripts" is correct. The official register
(INTF Kurzgefasste Liste) lists ~5,999 catalog numbers; after de-duplication Daniel
Wallace/CSNTM put the working figure at ~5,800 Greek MSS. Adding Latin/Coptic/Syriac/etc.
versional witnesses, the total across all languages is commonly given as ~20,000–25,000.
Slide-safe: **"5,800+ Greek manuscripts (20,000+ across all ancient languages)."**

**Old Testament — REPLACE.** The "17,000+ copies" figure is NOT defensible as stated and I
could not source it to any standard reference; it appears to be an inflated/garbled number.
What IS defensible:

- ~**220+ biblical manuscripts/fragments** among the Dead Sea Scrolls (c. 100 BC–AD 100),
  which pushed our oldest Hebrew text back ~1,000 years.
- The Dead Sea Scrolls match the medieval Masoretic text in **>95%** of the text, the
  variants being mostly spelling — i.e., the OT was transmitted with extreme fidelity.
- The cleanest large number, if a big figure is wanted: scholars often cite **~10,000+
  Hebrew Masoretic manuscripts** held in collections such as the Cairo Genizah; but on a
  slide it's safer to argue **fidelity over raw count**.

Recommended OT slide wording (drop "17,000"):

> **Old Testament: the Dead Sea Scrolls (c. 100 BC) match the medieval Hebrew text in over
> 95% of the words — 1,000 years of copying with near-perfect fidelity.**

**Homer's Iliad — REPLACE the number.** The deck's "643" is the old count. Current
scholarship (Graeme Bird and others) puts surviving Iliad manuscripts at **~1,900+**. Use
"~1,900" or simply "under 2,000" to stay safe.

**Caesar's Gallic Wars — KEEP with caveat.** The traditional bibliographic-test figure is
**~10 manuscripts** (with the earliest copy ~900–1,000 years after composition). Note:
updated catalogues count far more (one source cites 251). To avoid a "your number is
wrong" challenge, phrase as _"only a handful of early manuscripts, the oldest ~900 years
after Caesar"_ rather than asserting an exact small integer, OR explicitly say "≈10 early
manuscripts (traditional count)."

Slide-ready comparison block:

> **New Testament: 5,800+ Greek manuscripts; earliest within ~100 years of the events.**
> **Homer's Iliad (next best): ~1,900 manuscripts.**
> **Caesar's Gallic Wars: only a handful — the oldest copy ~900 years later.**
> No other ancient document is preserved like the New Testament.

[notes] Caution flag for a skeptic: the bibliographic test proves _textual integrity_ (we
have what was written), NOT _truth of contents_. Don't oversell — say "we can be confident
we're reading what the authors actually wrote," not "this proves it's true." That honesty
is itself persuasive.

**Sources**

  https://danielbwallace.com/tag/number-of-new-testament-manuscripts/ ; https://www.csntm.org/
- Christian Research Institute, "The Bibliographical Test Updated" (Iliad ~1,900; ancient
  comparisons) — https://www.equip.org/articles/the-bibliographical-test-updated/
- Biblical Archaeology Society, "Comparing Ancient Biblical Manuscripts" (DSS vs Masoretic

## 10. Hittites doubted, then confirmed (Boghazköy)

**VERDICT: REPLACE** (the core story is TRUE and excellent — but drop the "only source /
believed fabricated" overreach).

TRUE: For much of the 19th century skeptics doubted the Hittites existed; some said "no
such people ever existed" and used their absence to question the Bible. Archibald Sayce
proposed their reality (1876); **Hugo Winckler's 1906 excavation at Boğazköy (ancient
Hattusha)** uncovered the Hittite imperial capital and ~10,000 cuneiform tablets, placing
the empire beyond doubt.

OVERREACH TO AVOID: Do NOT say the Bible was "the only source" or that the Hittites were
"universally believed to be fabricated." Egyptian records (the treaty of Ramesses II with
the "Kheta") and the Amarna Letters (references to "Hatti") already pointed to them before
Winckler — the Bible was one witness among a few, not the sole one. The honest claim is
that critics _doubted_ the Hittites and used that doubt against Scripture, and archaeology
_vindicated_ the biblical references.

Slide-ready:

> **The Hittites: dismissed by 19th-century critics as a biblical invention — until Hugo
> Winckler excavated their lost capital (Boğazköy, 1906) and 10,000 cuneiform tablets. A
> people once called fiction turned out to rival Egypt and Babylon.**

**Sources**

- Apologetics Press, "Hidden Hittites" — https://apologeticspress.org/hidden-hittites-888/
- "Discovery of the Hittites," EpicArchaeology — https://epicarchaeology.org/2019/05/08/discovery-of-the-hittites/

## 11. Tel Dan Stele (1993, "House of David")

**VERDICT: KEEP** (verified; strong, mainstream-accepted evidence).

The Tel Dan Stele, discovered 1993 at Tel Dan, northern Israel (excavation dir. Avraham
Biran; found by Gila Cook), is a 9th-century-BC Old Aramaic victory inscription — most
scholars attribute it to **Hazael of Aram-Damascus** — that contains the phrase **"bytdwd"
= "House of David."** It is the earliest extrabiblical reference to the Davidic dynasty,
and notably it comes from an _enemy_ king, not Israelite scribes. Mainstream scholarly
consensus accepts the "House of David" reading.

Slide-ready:

> **The Tel Dan Stele (1993): a 9th-century-BC victory monument carved by an ENEMY king,
> naming "the House of David." The first hard evidence outside the Bible that David's
> dynasty was real.**

[notes] A handful of minimalist scholars once contested the reading; that's a minority and
the consensus is firmly for "House of David." Safe to present confidently.

**Sources**

- Biblical Archaeology Society, "The Tel Dan Inscription: The First Historical Evidence of
  King David" — https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/biblical-artifacts/the-tel-dan-inscription-the-first-historical-evidence-of-the-king-david-bible-story/
- "Tel Dan stele," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tel_Dan_stele

## 12. Pilate Stone (1961, Caesarea)

**VERDICT: KEEP** (verified).

Discovered June 1961 at Caesarea Maritima (excavation led by Antonio Frova; found reused as
a step in the Roman theatre). The Latin inscription names **[Pont]ius Pilatus** and his
title **[praef]ectus Iuda[ea]e** ("prefect of Judaea"), dating to AD 26–36. It is the only
contemporary inscription naming Pilate.

Slide-ready:

> **The Pilate Stone (Caesarea, 1961): a Roman inscription naming "Pontius Pilatus, Prefect
> of Judaea" — contemporary, stone-cut proof that the man who tried Jesus was real and held
> exactly that office.**

[notes] Nice bonus for a skeptic: the stone says "prefect," while later writers (and some
Gospel translations) call him "governor/procurator." The _earlier, contemporary_ title on
the stone matches the period precisely — a mark of authenticity, not contradiction.

## 14. "Christianity is the only religion that teaches the duty to share/teach others"

**VERDICT: DROP / REWORD** (factually false — Islam and Buddhism are both missionary
religions; a skeptic will dismantle this instantly).

Islam has **dawah** ("invitation"), widely held as a duty for Muslims. Buddhism launched
what scholars (Richard Foltz) call "the first large-scale missionary effort in world
religious history" under **Emperor Ashoka (3rd century BC)**. The standard religious-studies
taxonomy (since Max Müller, 1873) classes Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam together as the
three great _missionary_ religions. So "Christianity is the only religion that teaches the
duty to share" is simply wrong.

Defensible reword (if a point about the Bible's outward, universal mandate is wanted):

> **Christianity carries a universal mandate to teach all people (Matt 28:19) — a faith
> meant to be shared, not hoarded.**

[notes] Keep the claim ABOUT Christianity's own commission (which is true and Scripture-
backed) and DROP the comparative "only religion" claim. If a contrast is desired, the
honest one is narrower: e.g., that the Christian commission is explicitly _universal_ and
_non-coercive_ in its founding text — but even that needs care, since Islam's dawah also
claims non-coercion (Qur'an 2:256). Safest: make the positive claim about Christianity and
make no "only" claim about other religions.

**Sources**

- "Dawah," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dawah
- "Missionary," Wikipedia (Müller's missionary/non-missionary taxonomy; Ashoka; Buddhist
  missions) — https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missionary
- "Islamic missionary activity," Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_missionary_activity
