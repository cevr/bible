# The Reformers' Own Reasoning — and the Spiritualism Overlap

Compiled: 2026-06-06

Supporting documentation for **§IV.B(3a)** and **§IV.B(3b)** of `dress-reform.md`.

This file answers two questions the older `sex-distinction-historical-sources.md`
left open. That file established the **design lineage** (bloomer → "American
Costume" → pantsuit) and flagged the 20th-century "express purpose" claim as
inference. This file supplies what was missing:

1. **What the 19th-century reformers _themselves_ said** about why the skirt had
   to go, why woman's dress should approach man's, and how this bound to the
   woman's-rights cause — verified to digitized **primary texts**.
2. **The documented overlap between dress reform, woman's rights, and
   Spiritualism** — the evidentiary backbone for Ellen White's specific 1863
   claim that "Spiritualists have, to quite an extent, adopted this singular mode
   of dress" (1T 421.4 / 457).

Sources are marked **[SOLID]** (verified to a primary text or reputable
scholarship, citable as fact) or **[SHAKY — DO NOT HARD-QUOTE]** (widely reported
but not run to ground in an original source). This split is binding.

The two central primary documents — **Gerrit Smith's 1855 letter** and **Elizabeth
Cady Stanton's reply** — were extracted **verbatim** from the digitized full text
of _History of Woman Suffrage_, Vol. 1 (Internet Archive `historyofwomansu01stanuoft`,
pp. 836–842), and re-checked against the page images. The **1863 _Progressive
Annual_** roster was confirmed firsthand by direct fetch of the IAPSOP scan.

## PART A — THE REFORMERS' OWN REASONING

### A.1 [SOLID] Gerrit Smith — the skirt is the badge _and the cause_ of woman's bondage

Smith's open letter to his cousin Elizabeth Cady Stanton (the "Woman's Rights
Movement" letter, late 1855; Stanton's reply is dated Dec. 21, 1855), reproduced
in full in _History of Woman Suffrage_, Vol. 1 (New York: Fowler & Wells, 1881),
pp. 836–839. Signed "Affectionately yours, GERRIT SMITH."

- **Source (verified verbatim):**
  https://archive.org/details/historyofwomansu01stanuoft
  (full text https://archive.org/stream/historyofwomansu01stanuoft/historyofwomansu01stanuoft_djvu.txt,
  pp. 836–839); Project Gutenberg ebook #28020 https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/28020

This single source is the strongest evidence in the whole §IV.B file. Smith's
thesis is _not_ "the skirt is impractical" — it is that the skirt is the
**outgrowth, symbol, and reinforcing cause** of woman's degradation, and that
re-dressing toward man's freedom is the _battle-ground_ of her emancipation.

**Verified hard-quotes (safe to quote; OCR double-spaces collapsed):**

> "the woman's rights women — persevere just as blindly and stubbornly as do
> other women, in wearing a dress that **both marks and makes their impotence**."
> (p. 836)

> "I admit that the dress of woman is not the primal cause of her helplessness
> and degradation. **That cause is to be found in the false doctrines and
> sentiments of which the dress is the outgrowth and symbol.**" (p. 837)

> "Were woman to throw off the dress … and to put on a dress that would leave her
> free to work her own way through the world … **he would confess her
> transmutation into his equal**; and, therefore, all occasion for the display of
> chivalry and gallantry toward her on the one hand, and tyranny on the other,
> would have passed away." (p. 837)

> "I hazard nothing in saying, that **the relation between the dress and
> degradation of an American woman, is as vital as between the cramped foot and
> degradation of a Chinese woman**." (p. 837)

> "to concede to her the rights of property would be to benefit her comparatively
> little, unless she shall resolve to **break out from her clothes-prison**." (p. 837)

> "Strive as you will to elevate woman, nevertheless **the disabilities and
> degradation of this dress** … will make your striving vain." (p. 838)

> "where shall be the battle-ground for this indispensable self-conquest? She
> will laugh at my answer when I tell her, that **her dress, aye, her dress, must
> be that battle-ground**. … her triumph there will be her triumph everywhere; and
> that her failure there will be her failure everywhere." (p. 838)

**One honest caveat to carry into the study (Smith himself):** Smith did _not_
want the sexes' dress made identical. In the same letter he allows that "however
much the dresses of the sexes should resemble each other, decency and virtue and
other considerations require that they should be **obviously distinguishable** from
each other." So the goal is _resemblance toward man's freedom_, not literal
uniform — a distinction the study must keep, lest it overstate.

### A.2 [SOLID] Elizabeth Cady Stanton — "dress just like man"; sex is "a badge of degradation"

Stanton's reply to Smith, _History of Woman Suffrage_, Vol. 1, pp. 839–842, dated
"SENECA FALLS, Dec. 21, 1855," opening "MY DEAR COUSIN." Same archive.org source.
Here Stanton pushes Smith's logic _further_ than Smith himself: she argues from
the **identity of the sexes** ("man is woman and woman is man") to the conclusion
that the dress-distinction should be erased outright.

**Verified hard-quotes (safe to quote):**

> "Believing, as you do, in the identity of the sexes, that all the difference we
> see in tastes, in character, is entirely the result of education — that '**man is
> woman and woman is man**' — why keep up these distinctions in dress?" (p. 841)

> "to me, it seems that **if she would enjoy entire freedom, she should dress just
> like man. Why proclaim our sex on the house-tops, seeing that it is a badge of
> degradation**, and deprives us of so many rights and privileges wherever we go?"
> (p. 841)

> "Disguised as a man, the distinguished French woman, 'George Sand,' has been
> able to see life in Paris … In male attire, we could travel by land or sea …
> **If nature has not made the sex so clearly defined as to be seen through any
> disguise, why should we make the difference so striking?**" (p. 841)

> "when men and women in their every-day life **see and think less of sex** and
> more of mind, we shall all lead far purer and higher lives." (p. 841)

**Stanton's counter-weight (quote for honesty — she ranked _marriage law_ above
dress):** In the same reply she warns Smith against making dress the whole cause:

> "**A true marriage relation has far more to do with the elevation of woman than
> the style and cut of her dress.** … We have no reason to hope that pantaloons
> would do more for us than they have done for man himself. The negro slave
> enjoys the most unlimited freedom in his attire … yet … he is a slave still."
> (p. 841)

This caveat matters: the study must present "dress = badge of bondage, re-dress
toward man = emancipation" as **Smith's thesis, embraced and pushed by Stanton**,
not as a uniform movement creed. It is plenty for the argument — the two most
prominent voices in the exchange say exactly the thing EGW named — but it must not
be inflated into "every reformer said so."

### A.3 [SOLID] Stanton's autobiography — adopt-reasons and abandon-reasons

Stanton, _Eighty Years and More_ (1898), the bloomer passage. Verified verbatim
from Project Gutenberg ebook #11982:
https://www.gutenberg.org/files/11982/11982-h/11982-h.htm

> "**To him [Gerrit Smith] the whole revolution in woman's position turned on her
> dress. The long skirt was the symbol of her degradation.**"

> "the physical freedom enjoyed did not compensate for the persistent persecution
> and petty annoyances suffered at every turn." (her stated reason for
> _abandoning_ the dress — it was social cost, not a change of principle)

The parallel reminiscence in _History of Woman Suffrage_ Vol. 1 (p. 471) gives her
adopt-reason: "What a sense of liberty I felt … running up and down stairs with my
hands free … What an emancipation."

### A.4 [SOLID] Elizabeth Smith Miller (originator of the design)

Her own account (NYPL collection, transcribed at Assumption College's Women's
History site): http://www1.assumption.edu/whw/Smith_Miller_on_dress.html

> "I became so thoroughly disgusted with the long skirt, that the dissatisfaction
> … suddenly ripened into the decision that **this shackle should no longer be
> endured**."

Her father was Gerrit Smith, who gave the design "his full approval." NOTE: this
is a transcribed account of a manuscript — reliable, but treat as primary-via-
transcription. Miller's stated reasoning is more health/practicality than political.

### A.5 [SOLID for the title; SHAKY for body-text] Dr. Harriet N. Austin — "Woman's Right to Good Health"

Austin (Dansville "Our Home" water-cure) is the physician EGW named (5MR 380.4).
Her book was titled **_The American Costume, or Woman's Right to Good Health_** —
the **rights-claim is in the title itself**, and that title is **[SOLID]** (catalog
record, e.g. Google Books id `lEk7PwAACAAJ`:
https://books.google.com/books/about/The_American_Costume_Or_Woman_s_Right_to.html?id=lEk7PwAACAAJ).

- White Estate documentary page (Austin's 1863 _Laws of Life_ convention address,
  "Adopt the American Costume"): https://whiteestate.org/about/issues1/ref-lib/add-docs/dress/
- Biography (secondary): https://www.healthscience.org/harriet-n-austin-m-d-detailed-biography/

**[SHAKY — DO NOT HARD-QUOTE]:** No digitized full text of Austin's book was
located; her verified _own words_ are health-first, not an explicit "dress like
men / woman's rights" argument. The political reading of Austin rests on the book
**title** (solid) plus her place in the rights-movement network (solid), not on
verified body text. Do not put a "rights philosophy" quote in Austin's mouth
beyond the title until the book itself is read.

### A.6 [SOLID facts, SHAKY quotes] The Sibyl / Hasbrouck / NDRA

- _The Sibyl: A Review of the Tastes, Errors and Fashions of Society_ (Middletown,
  NY, 1856–1864), organ of the National Dress Reform Association, ed. Dr. Lydia
  Sayer Hasbrouck. Digitized issues: New York Heritage,
  https://nyheritage.org/collections/lydia-sayer-hasbrouck-dress-reform-collection
- National Dress Reform Association (founded 1856 by hydropath James Caleb
  Jackson; branded the dress "the American Costume"):
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Dress_Reform_Association ;
  https://freethought-trail.org/profiles/profile:national-dress-reform-association/

**Important nuance (carry into the study):** the NDRA pitched the dress as
**health**, and was "generally cool toward suffrage, arguing that a revolution in
women's apparel, not the vote, was what was most needed to make women free"
(Freethought Trail). So the NDRA channel echoes _Smith's_ "dress-first" position
more than a vote-first feminism — but it is framed as health, not "dress like
men." The Hasbrouck line that women could not "compete on equal terms with men so
long as they were hampered by" their clothing is a **[SHAKY]** paraphrase in the
secondary literature, not a verified quote. Pull from the digitized _Sibyl_ before
hard-quoting.

### A.7 [SHAKY — DO NOT HARD-QUOTE] Famous one-liners that did NOT verify

- **Amelia Bloomer**, "[women] have been and are slaves, while man in dress and
  all things else is free" (attributed to _The Lily_, Feb. 1851). Widely repeated
  (Rappaport; _History Today_; _Smithsonian_) but **not found** in any digitized
  _Lily_ issue or in the standard Bloomer biography; no source gives an
  issue/page locator.
  - Worth noting: Bloomer is actually a **counter-witness** to the "dress like
    men" goal. Verified from _Life and Writings of Amelia Bloomer_ (1895),
    Gutenberg #69953: "**We do not advocate the same style of dress, altogether,
    for both sexes and should be sorry to see women dress just like men.**" So the
    resemblance-to-men goal is Smith's and Stanton's, **not** Bloomer's — the
    study must not generalize it to "the reformers" flatly.
- **Stanton**, "We put the dress on for greater freedom, but what is physical
  freedom compared with mental bondage?" — **not** in _Eighty Years and More_;
  appears to be a paraphrase. Do not hard-quote.

## PART B — THE SPIRITUALISM OVERLAP (backbone of EGW's 1863 claim)

EGW wrote in **June 1863** (1T 421.4 / "Reform in Dress," _Testimonies_ vol. 1
pp. 421–422 / 456–457): "Spiritualists have, to quite an extent, adopted this
singular mode of dress." The question for the audit: does the record substantiate
that _specific factual claim_? It does — decisively.

### B.1 [SOLID — the centerpiece] The 1863 _Progressive Annual_ rostered American-Costume wearers in a Spiritualist almanac

The leading American Spiritualist publishing house — **A. J. Davis & Co., at the
office of _The Herald of Progress_** (Andrew Jackson Davis's Spiritualist
newspaper, New York) — published an annual register, the **_Progressive Annual_**
("Comprising an Almanac, a Spiritualist Register, and a General Calendar of
Reform"). It carried a recurring section headed **"Practical Dress Reformers."**

- **1863 edition — confirmed firsthand** (direct fetch):
  http://iapsop.com/spirithistory/1863_progressive_annual.html
  - Publisher: **A. J. Davis & Co.**, at "the Office of 'The Herald of Progress'"
    (a Spiritualist paper). **[SOLID]**
  - Section heading: **"Practical Dress Reformers."** Introductory note (verbatim):
    "The following is, we are assured, very far from being a complete list of
    those who have adopted and habitually wear the '**American Costume**,' or short
    skirt Reform Dress." **[SOLID]**
  - The roster contains **~80 named women**, with locations. **[SOLID]**
  - Adjacent reform categories in the same register include **"Woman's Rights
    Reformers," "Temperance and Health Reformers,"** and **"Practicing Women
    Physicians"** — Spiritualism, woman's rights, and dress reform rostered side
    by side in one Spiritualist yearbook. **[SOLID]**
- **1862 edition** (13 names under the same heading):
  http://iapsop.com/spirithistory/1862_progressive_annual.html
- **1864 edition** ("Dress Reformers" heading, roster omitted for space / lack of
  a complete list): http://iapsop.com/spirithistory/1864_progressive_annual.html

**The force of this:** the very Spiritualist movement EGW named was, _the same
year she wrote_, publicly cataloguing ~80 American-Costume wearers in its own
almanac. Her claim is not detached polemic; it tracks a self-advertised feature
of the Spiritualist reform network.

**CRITICAL disambiguation — do not let anyone conflate this:** the register lists
**"Harmon, Ellen Beard, Aurora, Ill."** — but she appears under **"Practicing
Women Physicians,"** _not_ the dress-reform roster, and she is a **separate Illinois
physician**, **NOT** Ellen G. (Harmon) White, the SDA co-founder. Confirmed
firsthand: Ellen G. White does **not** appear in the dress-reformer list. Flag
this in the study so no reader mistakes the two.

### B.2 [SOLID] Ann Braude, _Radical Spirits_ — the peer-reviewed scholarly anchor (exact pages verified)

**Ann Braude, _Radical Spirits: Spiritualism and Women's Rights in Nineteenth-
Century America_** (Boston: Beacon Press, 1989; 2nd ed., Bloomington: Indiana
University Press, 2001). 1st ed.: xiii, 268 pp. + 8 plates; LCCN 89042599; ISBN 0807075000.

- Borrowable full text + search-inside: https://archive.org/details/radicalspiritssp0000brau
- JSTOR (2nd ed.): https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv11hpthg

Braude's central, much-cited thesis: Spiritualism was "a major — if not the major
— vehicle for the spread of women's rights ideas in the mid-nineteenth century."
She documents that Spiritualists participated in all the radical reforms of the
century, **dress reform among them**. The dress-reform / woman's-rights /
Spiritualism nexus is one interlocking radical-reform network — exactly the
"spirit" EGW described as attending the movement.

**EXACT PAGE CITATIONS (verified firsthand via the Internet Archive search-inside
full-text index; quotations transcribed from the returned OCR snippets — re-check
against page images before letter-perfect print).** Her index entry reads
"**Dress reform, 79, 142, 151–53**"; the dedicated discussion runs **pp. 151–153**,
and the Spiritualist-dress-reform narrative concentrates at **pp. 177–179**:

- **p. 177** — Braude names the Spiritualist register and the costume by name:
  > "Andrew Jackson Davis's **Progressive Annual** listed not only Spiritualist
  > lecturers but also practicing women physicians, women instructors in light
  > gymnastics, '**practical dress reformers**' who wore the '**American costume**'
  > (composed, like the bloomer, of a short skirt over loose trousers), and
  > speakers on the freedom and equality of the sexes."
  > — This is **independent scholarly corroboration of B.1** (the 1862–63
  > _Progressive Annual_ roster), from a peer-reviewed monograph.
- **p. 178** — Davis's own Spiritualist paper made dress reform a freedom-claim:
  > "'**Dress reform will eradicate the worst slavery of American women**,' claimed
  > Andrew Jackson Davis's _Herald of Progress_. Dress reform applied only to
  > women, no attempt being made to alter the standard male costume."
- **p. 179** — the Spiritualists were the _most committed_ dress reformers, and
  the link to the suffrage leaders is exact:
  > "the practitioners of dress reform found a more receptive audience among
  > Spiritualists. … **Stanton and Anthony wore the bloomer briefly but soon gave
  > up the new costume because it attracted ridicule, not because they no longer
  > believed in the principles that caused them to adopt it.** … the small cadre of
  > Spiritualist dress reformers put principles before pragmatism. They continued
  > to wear and to advocate the reform dress long after most reformers abandoned
  > it. … The _Banner of Light_ and Spiritualist conventions provided forums for
  > discussion of dress reform between the demise of the National Dress Reform
  > Association in 1863 and the founding of the **American Free Dress League by
  > Spiritualist Mary Tillotson in 1875**."
- **p. 98** — "the additional ridicule heaped on those who wore the 'American
  Dress,' composed, like the bloomer costume, of a shortened skirt over loose
  trousers. … Spiritualists … kept woman's rights before the public during the
  Civil War years."

This **closes the one SHAKY link** that previously stood here: Braude names the
American Costume / bloomer and the _Progressive Annual_ explicitly, on pp. 98,
177–179 (dedicated dress-reform treatment pp. 151–153). Safe to hard-cite.

### B.3 [SOLID] Single-figure convergences (one person = all the threads)

- **Mary Gove Nichols** (1810–1884) — hydropathist, dress reformer, woman's-rights/
  health reformer, free-love advocate, **and spiritualist** (she and husband "both
  attended séances, believing themselves to be in communication with spirits").
  Primary anchor: her autobiography _Mary Lyndon_ (1860).
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Gove_Nichols ;
  https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mary-Gove-Nichols
- **Thomas Low Nichols** (1815–1901) — "He wrote and campaigned on free love,
  hydrotherapy, food and health reform, vegetarianism, **spiritualism**,
  temperance, **dress reform**…" https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._L._Nichols
- **Dr. Juliet Stillman Severance** — spoke on women's health and **dress reform
  at a Spiritualist convention in Chicago (1865)**; later president of state
  Spiritualist associations (IL, WI, MN). A Spiritualist leader advocating dress
  reform from a Spiritualist platform. **[SOLID]**
- **Susan Pecker Fowler** — in the Spiritualist _Progressive Annual_ dress-reform
  roster; later a leader of the New Jersey Association of Spiritualists and Friends
  of Progress and the Vineland Anti-Fashion Society; suffrage officer. **[SOLID]**
  https://discovernjhistory.org/njsuffragists-susan-pecker-fowler/
- **Andrew Jackson Davis** ("the Poughkeepsie Seer"), leading American Spiritualist
  philosopher — his firm _published_ the _Progressive Annual_ that rostered the
  American-Costume wearers; his Harmonial Philosophy embraced the full radical-
  reform slate. The institutional link (his house catalogued dress reformers) is
  **[SOLID]**; "Davis personally advocated _the dress_ in his own words" is
  **[SHAKY]** (a personal quote was not located).
- **Stephen Pearl Andrews** — co-founder of the Modern Times community (Long
  Island), "a leader in the religious movement of spiritualism"; the community was
  a free-love/spiritualist haven. Spiritualist leader + radical-reform community
  founder **[SOLID]**; bloomer-wearing _at_ Modern Times specifically **[SHAKY]**.

### B.4 [SHAKY — DO NOT HARD-QUOTE] Personal spiritualism of individual dress reformers

The _Progressive Annual_ proves the **Spiritualists listed/claimed** these women as
dress reformers; it does **not** prove each one personally held séances. So:

- **Harriet Austin, Lydia Sayer Hasbrouck, Mary E. Walker, Mary E. Tillotson** —
  appear in (or are tied to) the dress-reform network and, for several, the
  Spiritualist register; but a primary statement of each one's _own_ spiritualist
  belief was **not** located. Cross-listing in a Spiritualist almanac is SOLID;
  "she was a confessing spiritualist" is SHAKY for these individuals. Keep the
  claim at the level the evidence supports: _the Spiritualist movement embraced and
  rostered the dress; many dress reformers moved in Spiritualist circles_ — not
  "every named reformer was a spiritualist."

## BOTTOM LINE FOR THE STUDY

- **The reformers' own reasoning** (Smith, Stanton): the skirt was framed as the
  **badge and reinforcing cause of woman's subjection**, and re-dressing toward
  man's freedom as the **battle-ground of emancipation** — verified verbatim. This
  is precisely "the doing away of the distinction of dress between males and
  females" EGW condemned (1T 459.8) — stated by the reformers themselves, as a
  goal. Caveat: it is Smith's-and-Stanton's thesis, not a uniform movement creed
  (Bloomer dissented; NDRA/Austin pitched health).
- **EGW's spiritualism claim** is **well-founded**: the leading Spiritualist house
  published a named roster of ~80 American-Costume wearers in 1863, the same year
  she wrote; and Braude's scholarship confirms dress reform, woman's rights, and
  Spiritualism were one radical-reform network. Use the _Progressive Annual_ roster
  as the centerpiece; keep individuals' personal spiritualism in the SHAKY column;
  never conflate the roster's "Ellen Beard Harmon" with Ellen G. White.
