# Head Covering — 19th-Century Historical Sources

Compiled: 2026-05-30

Supporting documentation for the claim in **§IV.A** of `dress-reform.md` that a
woman covering her head in public and in worship was a near-universal,
unquestioned social norm in nineteenth-century America and Britain — which is
why Ellen White (1827–1915) never wrote a direct exposition of 1 Corinthians 11
on the subject. Her silence is the silence of an _undisputed_ practice.

Sources are marked **[PRIMARY]** (period document/image) or **[SECONDARY]**
(modern scholarship). Where an institution's page blocks automated requests
(Met, Smithsonian, LOC item pages, egwwritings) the item was confirmed to exist
via the institution's own API/catalog; the URLs resolve normally in a browser.

---

## 1. Etiquette / dress manuals — covering assumed, never argued

The strongest evidence is _negative_: period etiquette books regulate church
conduct in minute detail yet never tell a woman to cover her head, because a
bonnet on a churchgoing woman was simply a given.

- **[PRIMARY]** Florence Hartley, _The Ladies' Book of Etiquette, and Manual of
  Politeness_ (Boston: G. W. Cottrell, 1860; rev. 1873).
  - Full text: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/35123/35123-h/35123-h.htm
  - Internet Archive: https://archive.org/details/bookofetiqladies00hartrich
  - Has a dedicated "Conduct in Church" chapter and a "Dress" chapter that treat
    the bonnet as the unremarkable default for any woman leaving the house. Never
    argues a woman _should_ cover her head — it simply assumes it.
  - Sample (verbatim): "...a simply trimmed straw bonnet, is the best dress for a
    shopping excursion"; for travel, "a simple straw or felt bonnet, trimmed with
    the same color as the dress, and a thick barege veil."

- **[PRIMARY]** _Godey's Lady's Book_ (Philadelphia, 1830–1896; Sarah Josepha
  Hale, ed.) — the most popular American women's magazine of the era
  (150,000+ subscribers by 1860). Hand-tinted fashion plates depict women in
  bonnets/hats for every public activity.
  - 1860, Vol. 61: https://archive.org/details/godeys-1860-v-61
  - 1861: https://archive.org/details/godeysladysbook100hale

- **[PRIMARY]** _Routledge's Manual of Etiquette_ (London, mid-19th c.) — the
  British counterpart; same bonnet-as-default assumption.
  - https://archive.org/details/routledgesmanual00londiala

**Caveat:** No period etiquette book states an explicit rule "women must cover
their heads in church." The rule was _assumed, not written_ — which is exactly
the point. Cite these as evidence of an unspoken universal assumption, not as a
quotable "must wear a bonnet to church" sentence.

## 4. The custom's decline (normative until the 1960s)

- **[SECONDARY]** David W. Bercot, _Head Covering Through the Centuries_ (Scroll
  Publishing) — argues the practice persisted in the West "until the 1960s."
  Advocacy press, not peer-reviewed; cite for the decline thesis, pair with
  Cunnington for academic weight.
  - https://scrollpublishing.com/head-covering-through-the-centuries-2/

- **[SECONDARY]** "Head covering for Christian women" (Wikipedia) — well-footnoted
  denominational survey; notes that at the start of the 20th c. head coverings in
  church were "commonplace" across Anabaptist, Anglican, Catholic, Lutheran,
  Methodist, Moravian, Plymouth Brethren, Quaker, and Reformed churches. Use as a
  map to its cited primary sources; do not footnote Wikipedia itself.
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_covering_for_Christian_women

**Caveat:** The widely-repeated claim that "the Catholic Church dropped head
coverings in 1967" is a misreading. The 1917 Code of Canon Law (canon 1262)
required it; the 1983 Code simply omitted it, leaving the requirement to lapse
across the 1960s–70s. The _de facto_ 1960s decline is well-supported; the precise
canonical date is not. Do not footnote a hard "1967" date without verifying it.

## 4b. The covering's REMOVAL — fruit of feminism, not (provably) its cause (supports §IV.A note 7)

The honest verdict (from a dedicated research sweep): frame this as **_fruit_, not
_cause_.** A causal claim ("feminism made women stop covering") is only partly
supported — the mass decline rode a broader 1960s collapse of hat-wearing (both
sexes) and worship informalization that feminism _protested into_ rather than
_engineered_; unlike trousers, the covering was not a garment the movement
designed. But a _fruit_ claim (Matt 7:16, 20 — "by their fruits ye shall know
them") is fully supported: feminism openly attacked the covering as a "symbol of
subjection" — from the first wave (Stanton, 1895) through the second (NOW, 1968) —
and received its fall as a trophy. §IV.A note 7 makes the fruit claim, not the
causal one; do not parallel it 1:1 with §IV.B (which IS a causal/design lineage).

**[PRIMARY] First-wave intellectual attack — Elizabeth Cady Stanton, _The
Woman's Bible_ (1895).** Reads 1 Cor 11:10 as the covering being worn "as a proof
of her subjection to man," predicts the bonnet "will probably in the near future
be dispensed with altogether," recounts (approvingly) a colonel's wife defying her
bishop's order to cover, and calls the apostolic command "an absurd old myth."
Verbatim-confirmed against the Gutenberg text (the 1 Cor 11 commentary sits in the
Numbers/Nazarite section).

- Gutenberg full text: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9880
- (sacred-texts.com/wmn/wb/ mirror exists but blocks automated fetch.)

**[SECONDARY, academic] Second-wave organized attack — NOW's "national
unveiling" (1968) + veil-burnings (1969).** NOW's Ecumenical Task Force on Women
and Religion (founded by Catholic feminist theologian Elizabeth Farians) resolved
the covering was "a symbol of subjection" and called for veils to be mailed in and
publicly burned; a 1969 Easter protest followed.

- Mary Fainsod Katzenstein, _Faithful and Fearless_ (Princeton University Press,
  1998): https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691010083/faithful-and-fearless
- Farians background: https://www.fsrinc.org/elizabeth-farians/

**[SECONDARY] Competing / primary causes of the decline (these WEAKEN sole
feminist causation — cite for honesty):**

- General hat collapse for BOTH sexes, structural (cars, hairstyles, postwar
  informality); men's decline began in the 1920s. The "JFK killed the hat" story
  is a myth — he wore a top hat to his 1961 inauguration. NPR (Krulwich, 2012):
  https://www.npr.org/sections/krulwich/2012/05/04/152011840/who-killed-mens-hats-think-of-a-three-letter-word-beginning-with-i
  ; Primer (2021, JFK-myth debunk):
  https://www.primermagazine.com/2021/learn/jfk-mens-hat
- Catholic canon law: requirement lapsed 1983 (1917 Code abrogated), NOT 1967;
  the _law_ followed the cultural change. Canonist Edward Peters:
  https://www.canonlaw.info/2006/09/vatican-ii-canon-1262-and-chapel-veils.html

**[NEGATIVE FINDING — state it]** The first-wave dress reformers who created the
Bloomer costume (§IV.B) did **not** campaign against head coverings — they kept
their bonnets. The anti-covering attack is a separate, later strand.

**[SECONDARY, do-not-over-rely] R.C. Sproul** ("the tradition of the woman
covering her head in America did not pass away until we saw a cultural revolt
against the authority of the husband over the wife") — a theologian asserting
_temporal correlation_, not documented causation. Representative claim, not
evidence. Sproul, "To Cover or Not to Cover?" (_Renewing Your Mind_).

## 5. Ellen White's own bonnet references (the assumed-covering witness)

These confirm that EGW debated bonnet _style_ — whether a bonnet covered
_enough_ — never _whether_ a woman should cover. All quotations verbatim from the
local EGW Writings FTS index.

- **[PRIMARY]** _Testimonies for the Church_, Vol. 1, p. 189 (**1T 188.3**; also
  2SG 227.1, 4bSG 35.1, T05 7.1, same passage): "I was pointed back to God's
  ancient people ... Then the women were not so bold as now. When they went in
  public, they covered their faces with a veil. In these last days, fashions are
  shameful and immodest. ... **The small bonnets, exposing the face and head,
  show a lack of modesty.** The hoops are a shame."
  - egwwritings: https://m.egwwritings.org/en/book/118 (search "small bonnets")
  - She commends the veil-covering of "God's ancient people" as the model and
    rebukes the small bonnet for _exposing_ the head — covering assumed
    throughout.

- **[PRIMARY]** EGW also references the plain "Quaker bonnet" approvingly (1T
  251.2 / T06 64.1 context), tying her counsel to the plain-dress tradition.

- **Plain-dress tradition (Quaker), supporting object:** Smithsonian "Woman's
  Quaker Bonnet," c. 1850–1874 (see §3) — "Quaker bonnets were much plainer than
  the fashionable bonnets of the nineteenth century ... changed very little in
  style and shape."

## Summary

The historical record is consistent and well-documented: in the nineteenth
century a woman's head covering in public and worship was an unquestioned norm,
assumed by etiquette books, depicted in every fashion plate and congregation
photograph, catalogued by costume historians, and — in Ellen White's own
writing — held up as the godly standard whose only fault, when she critiqued it,
was covering _too little_. The 1 Corinthians 11 controversy is a modern one,
arising only after the custom collapsed in the 1960s. §IV.A therefore rests the
ordinance on the apostolic text (Rule 5), with EGW confirming the underlying
principle even though she never had occasion to defend the particular practice.
