# Speaking as Christ Spoke

Ellen G. White's counsel on the voice, arranged by practice for Cristian's preaching and narration.

**Sources.** I read _The Voice in Speech and Song_ (VSS) in full and cite each counsel to the source VSS draws on, every one confirmed by lookup. Her main passages are 6T 380–383 and 4T 404–405 (reprinted as GW 86–91), COL 335–337, CT 238–247 and 253–255, Ed 198–199 and Ev 665–670. **Your measure** lines draw on the study of nine of your sermons (March–June 2026), which measured words, sentences and speed, not pitch, loudness, breath or gestures.

## 1. How Christ spoke

- His voice was musical and never strained. He did not crowd His words, and He spoke each one distinctly (RH March 5, 1895, par. 4).
- He spoke slowly and leaned on the words that mattered. His hearers took in each sentence before the next one began (CT 240.1; 6LtMs, Ms 19b, 1890, par. 47).
- He kept His natural key, and with it the feeling and melody of the voice (6LtMs, Ms 25, 1890, par. 8; 2T 615.1; 2T 617.3).
- He was earnest rather than vehement, with calm authority and no doubt (DA 254.1; MB 46.4; DA 253.5).
- Love showed in His look and tone, and there were tears in His voice even when He rebuked. He watched His hearers' faces as He spoke (DA 254.4; GW 117.1; DA 255.1).

> "He spoke slowly and impressively, emphasizing those words to which He wished His hearers to give special heed." (CT 240.1)

**In practice:** After each sermon, play back one minute and score it on five marks: musical, unstrained, uncrowded, every word distinct, and calm with earnestness.

## 2. Why the voice matters

- Speech is a talent. With the voice we convince and persuade (COL 335.2).
- For some hearers, the speaker's manner decides whether the message is received. Of two men preaching the same Scriptures, the one who controls his voice leaves the better impression (COL 336.3; 12LtMs, Ms 4, 1897, par. 1).
- We do the training, with Jesus as the pattern. Then we ask the Spirit to do what we cannot (4T 405.3; COL 336.2).

> "why should we not educate ourselves to come just as near to the Pattern as it is possible for us to do" (2T 617.3)

**In practice:** Treat voice work as part of sermon preparation. Do one drill a day from sections 3–6, and check one take a week against the card at the end.

## 3. Pace and pauses

- Speak slowly. Hurried words lose their effect, and the hearer has no time to take each one in (CT 254.5; SpTA07 9.2).
- Fast speech can and should be corrected. Build the habit of speaking slowly, yet earnestly and solemnly, and let the Holy Spirit give the truth its force (ST September 13, 1899, par. 5; 12LtMs, Ms 4, 1897, par. 2; SpTA07 10.1).
- Let each sentence land before you start the next. Racing ahead to keep up the inspiration is not inspiration (6LtMs, Ms 19b, 1890, par. 47).
- She once preached with three interpreters following her, untroubled, praying that the Lord would speak to the hearers' hearts (4LtMs, Lt 23, 1885, par. 7; 4LtMs, Ms 77, 1886, par. 13).

> "When you rush one word right upon another, half the power is taken out of your discourse." (9LtMs, Lt 8, 1894, par. 3)

**Your measure:** This counsel asks the most change of you. Within a phrase you speak at 197–229 words a minute; BibleProject's narrators speak at 179–193. The interpreter's pauses give time between phrases, and the counsel asks for slowness within them too. She gives no figure.

**In practice:** Each day, time one Scripture paragraph read aloud. Reread it, sounding every syllable, until it nears your film rule of 150–165 words a minute. In every take, breathe between sentences (4T 404.1).

## 4. Distinctness, emphasis and repetition

- Make every word full and round, and keep your voice up to the last word of the sentence. Dropping it at the end loses the thought (6T 383.1; CT 246.1).
- Sound every syllable, and put the emphasis where it belongs (CT 254.5; 4T 405.1).
- Make your main points as clear as mileposts, and go over them again and again. Christ taught a few things at a time and repeated familiar truths (2T 616.2; GW 168.1; 6LtMs, Ms 25, 1890, par. 9).
- Give each repetition a fresh form. Christ's truth came in new representations, not in repeated words and familiar figures (RH January 7, 1890, par. 4).

> "Words that are worth speaking at all are worth speaking in a clear, distinct voice, with emphasis and expression." (6T 383.1)

**Your measure:** Your refrain is the repetition she asks for: in one sermon you named justification as faith, forgiveness and power four times. Repeating a reference up to four times while the room finds it serves the room, not the point. Sentence endings were not measured.

**In practice:** Say each reference once, slowly and in full, and let the interpreter carry it. Say it once more as you begin to read. On playback, check that your last words are as clear as your first.

## 5. Pitch, key and loudness

- Keep your natural key and the natural tones of your voice (6LtMs, Ms 25, 1890, par. 8; 2T 672.1).
- A solemn, well-pitched, modulated voice makes a better impression than shouting, and a lower tone saves strength (2T 615.1; 2T 616.2).
- Be heard without strain; a voice sunk too low to hear is no sign of humility. On the mount, Christ's words were neither whispered nor harsh (SpTA07 9.2; 6T 383.2; 7T 269.5).
- Let God's power show through the truth rather than through noise or loud, hurried speech. She herself talked too loud when she was young (1T 645.1; 6LtMs, Ms 19b, 1890, par. 47).

> "The tones of the voice have much to do in affecting the hearts of those that hear." (2T 615.1)

**Your measure:** Not measured.

**In practice:** Say a line the way you would to one friend at the table. Then give it to the room in the same key, adding breath but not pitch (2T 616.1). On playback, check whether your pitch rises in the loudest minute.

## 6. Breath and the body

- Stand erect, because good breathing follows good posture (Ed 198.3; Ed 198.4).
- Take a full breath at every sentence and push the words from the abdomen, with the throat only the channel. From that depth you can address thousands as easily as ten (4T 404.1; Ed 199.1; CT 297.2; 2T 616.1).
- Use more air and fewer words. Take time to breathe instead of crowding the words together (19LtMs, Lt 367, 1904, par. 3; 2T 615.2).

> "Stand straight, breathe deep, and speak your words with as much force as you please." (19LtMs, Lt 367, 1904, par. 3)

**Your measure:** Not measured.

**In practice:** Before you speak, stand erect and take five slow breaths that move the abdomen, not the shoulders. Then read a paragraph aloud, with a full breath at each sentence. Keep at it until it is a habit (Ed 198.4; CT 239.2).

## 7. Tone and spirit

- A calm, unhurried, earnest manner does more good than excitement (2T 672.1; 12LtMs, Ms 4, 1897, par. 1).
- Let the truth carry the force. Working yourself up to a passion is no proof of God's power or of zeal (SpTA07 10.1; 4T 405.1).
- Keep calm from turning cold. She retells an actor's rebuke: preachers speak of real things coldly, as if imaginary. Let your voice carry deep feeling (17LtMs, Lt 103, 1902, par. 7; CT 253.3).
- When you reprove, put all of Christ's tenderness into your voice. Be deeply earnest, with no harsh or loud note and no whining show of humility (Ev 174.2; 6T 66.4; 2T 617.1).

> "Into what you say put the spirit and life of Christ." (CT 254.5)

**Your measure:** You don't exclaim; your wonder is a plain fact, held for a moment. That is earnest rather than vehement (DA 254.1). The risk on the other side is coldness. Tone was not measured; the study found your tenderness mainly in confession and prayer.

**In practice:** Before you speak a hard truth, ask whether it will sound like reproof or like tears (GW 117.1). When you record alone, speak with as much earnestness as if thousands were listening (GW 167.3).

## 8. Plain words, short sentences, few points

- Choose plain words that need no dictionary, never words picked to sound learned. A child once asked her for easy words in place of _justification_ and _sanctification_ (GW 49.3; CT 240.2; 6T 383.1; CT 254.2).
- Use self-evident figures from daily life, and come straight to the point. Christ reached the heart through the imagination (CT 253.3; DA 254.2).
- Speak in short sentences, without numbered heads, as John the Baptist did. Teach by asking questions in a conversational tone (2SM 148.3; 18LtMs, Ms 41, 1903, par. 3).
- Make a few essential points and stop while interest is high. Short sermons are remembered better (5LtMs, Lt 39, 1887, par. 10; 3T 419.3; GW 168.1).
- Use sound words, not cheap expressions. Leave stories told to amuse, and lines meant only to raise a laugh, outside the pulpit (CT 238.2; TM 142.2; 1T 648.2).

> "pure native eloquence was revealed in the short sentences" (2SM 148.3)

**Your measure:** This is your closest match. Your median sentence runs 4–10 words, a quarter of your sentences are questions, and plain likenesses carry your doctrine. You define _justification_ in three plain words, the clear explanation CT 254.4 asks for. Two checks:

- "All right" (61 times) and "Let's go" (55) open many of your sentences. When "Let's go" names a text, it does a job. A bare "All right" is a place for fewer words and more air.
- Your humour is small and aimed at our own habits. It fits the counsel when it makes the point, not when it only draws the laugh.

Your part of each stream ran 40–96 minutes, interpretation included; where interest peaked was not measured.

**In practice:** Replace each "All right" with a silent breath (19LtMs, Lt 367, 1904, par. 3). In each outline, mark where interest should peak, and close soon after (3T 419.3).

## 9. Reading Scripture aloud

- Read distinctly and give the sense, as the Levites did, so the text keeps its force (COL 335.3; COL 335.4).
- Read with a soft, musical cadence, clearly, with emphasis where the sense needs it (6T 380.4; 16LtMs, Ms 85, 1901, par. 22).
- Voice and emphasis can make a scene as clear as sight. At about eleven she heard a minister read Peter's imprisonment so that it seemed to pass before her eyes; he had learned by talking to trees and oxen (6T 380.3; 18LtMs, Ms 91, 1903, par. 30).
- Read fluently, every word correct and clear (4T 405.4).

> "make the scene presented stand out as clearly before the mind of the listener as if it could actually be seen" (6T 380.3)

**Your measure:** Your reading aloud is in the word counts but was not measured on its own.

**In practice:** The day before, read the text aloud alone, as that minister practised with his oxen. Mark the one or two words that carry the sense, and put the emphasis there (CT 254.5).

## 10. Prayer and singing in public

- Pray so that all can hear and join: slowly, distinctly, head lifted (6T 383.3; 6T 381.4; CT 241.2).
- Keep public prayer to a few minutes and to the point, in a natural posture and a living, varied tone (GW 179.2; 2T 617.2; RH January 14, 1902, Art. A, par. 11).
- Pray aloud where only God hears; private prayer makes public prayer clear (RH April 22, 1884, Art. A, par. 2).
- Sing for clear tone, correct pronunciation and distinct words, not volume: soft, natural and melodious, like the birds (9T 143.3; 18LtMs, Ms 91, 1903, par. 34).

> "speak slowly and distinctly and in tones loud enough to be heard by all so that the people may unite in saying, Amen" (6T 383.3)

**Your measure:** The study recorded what your opening prayers ask (that your words come from God's throne), not their sound or length; singing was not measured.

**In practice:** Pray the opening prayer aloud alone that morning. In the service, keep it to a few minutes, head up, pausing for the interpreter so the room can say Amen.

## 11. Gestures and manner

- The closer a man walks with God, the more fitting his manner and gestures become (4T 405.2; CT 238.2).
- Let your words, life and spirit carry the teaching, as Christ's did, more than gestures (CT 399.3). Keep gestures quiet, since violent ones weaken the truth's impression. Stand as a teacher, not a performer (Ev 184.1; Ev 640.1).
- Use your own gestures, tones and expressions, copied from no other man (13LtMs, Ms 104, 1898, par. 19).
- Speaking calmly may feel at first like a loss of freedom. Let that feeling go (RH February 5, 1880, par. 7).

> "You are to stand in your own armor, with your own phase of character, sanctified by God." (6LtMs, Ms 19b, 1890, par. 9)

**Your measure:** Not measured, since the study used transcripts only. Shape your manner after Christ's, not after another preacher's or narrator's.

**In practice:** Once a month, watch ten minutes of one of your sermons with the sound off. Note any gesture the words would not miss (CT 399.3).

## 12. Daily speech

- Train your voice at home and in all conversation: pleasant tones, kind words, and control when something goes wrong (COL 336.4; 6T 381.3).
- When talk turns unprofitable, quietly change the subject (COL 337.3).
- Home is the best school of speech, and it is never too late to improve (COL 337.4; 6T 381.5).

> "We should accustom ourselves to speak in pleasant tones, to use pure and correct language, and words that are kind and courteous." (COL 336.4)

**Your measure:** Not measured.

**In practice:** Use the family table to practise the slow pace and natural key from sections 3 and 5.

## Weekly practice

Check each line on the playback of a sermon or a take.

1. **Breath.** Erect; a full breath at each sentence, from the abdomen, throat open. (4T 404.1; CT 297.2)
2. **Pace.** Every syllable sounded; each sentence lands before the next. One minute timed inside phrases, against 150–165. (CT 254.5; 6LtMs, Ms 19b, 1890, par. 47)
3. **Endings.** The last word of each sentence as full as the first. (6T 383.1)
4. **Key.** The natural key held through the loudest minute; force from breath, not pitch. (6LtMs, Ms 25, 1890, par. 8; 2T 616.1)
5. **Words.** A breath where "All right" was; each reference said once, slowly, and once more at the reading. (CT 238.2; 19LtMs, Lt 367, 1904, par. 3)
6. **Points.** A few points, each a milepost, each repeated; stopped while interest was high. (2T 616.2; GW 168.1; 3T 419.3)
7. **Spirit.** Calm and earnest; the hardest line heard back for tenderness; an empty room addressed as if thousands were present. (2T 672.1; GW 117.1; GW 167.3)

## In the scripts

Every script in every series is written and marked so that reading it aloud practises this counsel (owner, 2026-10-14: "as part of the scripts for all our planned series"). The lab's staging voice reads the marks, so a draft can be heard before it is recorded.

- **One idea a sentence, the stressed word last,** and the voice kept up through it (6T 383.1; CT 240.1). The stress mark goes on the word that carries the sense (CT 254.5).
- **A breath mark at every sentence;** a timed hold only where the script means one (4T 404.1; 6LtMs, Ms 19b, 1890, par. 47). Paced at 150–165 words a minute (CRAFT rule 9).
- **A reference said once,** by its speaker or setting, never chapter and verse (CRAFT rule 2).
- **Repetition is the refrain's, in a fresh form each time,** never the same words for filler (RH January 7, 1890, par. 4).
- **A light line makes the point or goes** (TM 142.2).
- **The narrator records alone, as if thousands were present** (GW 167.3).
